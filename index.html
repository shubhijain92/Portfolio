A-07b · Leadership

How I led it.

On Construction, that meant replacing the requested schedule tables with five key numbers and the 3D floor stack — and walking directors through why a floor beats a percentage.

The pushback · what was asked for → what I argued for

Requested

12–15 KPIs on one screen

Data-heavy tables as the main view

A Power BI-style reporting dashboard

→

Shipped

5 key KPIs, everywhere

The same information as visual charts and graphs

An action centre: the user first, the right information one tap away

The rhythm · how the team ran

Daily

Morning status from the design interns: yesterday’s work, blockers.

2–3× a week

I sat with each intern to review their work and guide the changes.

Weekly

Sprint call with the PM and 3 developers: scope, trade-offs, handoff.

At ~50%

Demo to senior management and BD; their feedback went into the next sprint.

How research ran

Site shadowingStakeholder interviewsSpreadsheet & report auditStatus-call sit-insWhatsApp thread reviewPrototype usability testsDefinition workshops

Informal and built into delivery rather than a separate study — mostly stakeholder-led, and run alongside sprints.

3 site visits · 5 rounds of online sessions with site managers, PMs and BD · 2 rounds of usability testing

What didn’t work

AI data mapping nearly shipped without guardrails.

Users upload a lot of Excel, and the first mapping mis-read too much of it. We held it back, added validations, checks and a few deliberate manual steps — and reached about 85% correct results.

The hard constraint

Real data was scattered — or never captured.

Half arrived as PDFs, half as Excel; on construction sites much lived only on WhatsApp or calls. I designed on documented assumptions and treated them as the base, to be replaced as real data came in.

AI signal

70% acceptance in usability testing.

BD and engineers accepted the AI features at 70% after testing — once each one showed its evidence and left the final call to a person.

Theme 01

Eligibility scares people

“Am I allowed to do this?”

— Learner, via advisor · paraphrased

Saw: Most help calls started at eligibility questions.

So: Answer “do I qualify?” early, in plain words.

Theme 02

ID and address too soon

“I don’t have my documents on me.”

— Learner · paraphrased

Saw: Drop-offs spiked where ID and address were required.

So: Defer them until after sign-up.

Illustrative

Theme 03

Help arrives too late

“I only find out they’re stuck when they ring.”

So: Offer a call-back before people leave.

What didn’t work

The chat-style form felt gimmicky.

It was friendly, but editing an answer was painful and it slowed people down. We kept its tone and moved back to simple steps.

The hard constraint

Compliance still needed every detail.

Funding rules required ID, address and employment status — so the design deferred them to after sign-up, with advisors helping where needed.

Navigation model

From ad to enrolment

Clicks the ad→Step 1 · course→Step 2 · name→Hesitates?→Step 3 · contact→Advisor calls→Enrolled

Decision point — If someone pauses or tries to leave, a call-back popup offers to have an advisor ring them — turning a drop-off into an enquiry.

Edge cases

Leaves mid-way

Progress is saved; the call-back catches them.

Unsure about eligibility

Plain-language examples, and “we’ll check for you”.

No documents

Never asked at sign-up; advisors collect later.

Low data

Light pages, no heavy images in the form.
